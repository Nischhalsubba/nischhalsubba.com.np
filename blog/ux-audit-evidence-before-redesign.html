What Evidence Should a UX Audit Collect Before a Redesign?

A redesign becomes more defensible when the audit collects evidence from real behavior, real questions, and real product constraints rather than relying on taste.

By Nischhal Raj SubbaJuly 25, 20266 min read

A UX audit is only as useful as the evidence behind its findings. A redesign recommendation based on personal preference may sound confident, but it is difficult to defend, prioritize, or measure.

Before redesign work begins, collect enough evidence to explain three things: what users are trying to do, where the current product makes that difficult, and why the problem deserves attention now.

Quick answer

A useful UX audit should combine behavioral data, support patterns, interface-state review, accessibility checks, and stakeholder context. No single source is complete. The goal is to connect repeated evidence to a specific user task and business consequence.

1. Behavioral evidence

Start with what users actually do. Depending on the product, this may include funnel drop-off, completion rate, time on task, repeated retries, abandoned forms, unused features, or frequent navigation backtracking.

Analytics do not explain motivation by themselves, but they reveal where the product deserves closer inspection. A high drop-off rate on account verification is more useful than a general feeling that onboarding looks outdated.

2. Repeated support and sales questions

Support tickets, chat transcripts, onboarding calls, and sales objections often contain plain-language descriptions of product friction. Group repeated questions by task rather than by individual customer.

Examples include: “Where do I export this?”, “Why can’t I approve the payment?”, “What does this permission allow?”, or “Did my form submit?” Each repeated question points toward missing context, weak feedback, hidden actions, or confusing terminology.

3. Real interface states

Review more than the ideal default screen. Capture loading, empty, error, disabled, success, permission-restricted, offline, and partially completed states.

This is where visually polished products often break down. The default dashboard may look excellent while an empty result gives no explanation or an error message offers no recovery. The article Why Beautiful Interfaces Can Still Have Poor UX explains these failure patterns in more detail.

4. Accessibility evidence

Check keyboard operation, focus visibility, text and control contrast, target size, form labels, heading structure, and whether meaning depends on color alone.

Accessibility findings should be tied to affected tasks. “Focus style missing” becomes more actionable when the audit explains that keyboard users cannot see which billing control is active.

5. Product and technical context

A strong audit records constraints before recommending changes. Ask which workflows are business-critical, which components are shared, where technical debt exists, what is already planned, and what the team can realistically change.

This prevents recommendations that are theoretically elegant but operationally useless, a popular genre of consulting output.

Turn evidence into findings

For each significant finding, document:

  • the user task;
  • the observed problem;
  • the evidence source;
  • the likely consequence;
  • the affected users or frequency;
  • the recommended direction;
  • the confidence level and open questions.

Then rank the findings rather than treating every inconsistency as equally urgent. The UX Audit Prioritization Framework provides a practical method for deciding what to fix first.

A compact evidence checklist

  • Analytics for the most important flows
  • Recent support, sales, and onboarding questions
  • Screenshots of default and non-default states
  • Keyboard and basic accessibility review
  • Mobile behavior and content extremes
  • Business priorities and technical constraints
  • Examples that connect the problem to a real task

Final recommendation

Do not begin a redesign with a folder of attractive references and a complaint that the product feels old. Begin with evidence that shows where people struggle, what the product fails to communicate, and which problems matter most.
